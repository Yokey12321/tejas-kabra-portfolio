## System overview

This project started as a Drone Challenge UAV build for Gulf Coast Texas. The work covered the airframe, camera link, radio-control path, Betaflight setup, wiring, CAD layout, and payload rules.

The final prototype used a carbon-fiber drone base, brushless motor layout, Li-ion battery pack, camera, radio/controller hardware, an Arduino bridge, and a flight controller running Betaflight on an STM32F411 microcontroller.

## Wiring, packaging, and integration

The wiring work centered on making the flight controller, power distributor, radio hardware, camera, and Arduino physically fit inside the airframe while still remaining serviceable. The build log shows the process of organizing wire bundles, planning the layout, testing connections through the power distributor and flight controller, then zip-tying the final harness.

A practical issue appeared during integration: one connection was difficult to solder cleanly in the available space, so the team changed the physical attachment approach and moved to a more workable connection method for the prototype.

## Flight-control and camera software

The flight controller was configured in Betaflight, which handled the motor setup, gyro behavior, PID controller tuning, and general flight-control configuration. The project notes call out an STM32F411 microcontroller on the flight controller.

The Arduino acted as a bridge between the physical controller and the flight controller. The deck describes the Arduino translating signals from the receiver into flight-controller commands, which allowed the drone to be piloted remotely while keeping the software stack flexible.

Camera integration added another embedded layer. The project used Arduino code with `Wire.h` for I2C communication, sending camera information through the Arduino and radio path to the driver's visual setup. The deck also documents a webcam-to-radio mounting process, including validating the camera on a computer before stripping and integrating wires into the drone.

## Materials and constraints

The prototype materials list totals approximately $180. The largest cost was the carbon-fiber drone base at $125, followed by the camera at $35, extra wires at $11, and Li-ion batteries at $9. The Arduino, controller/radio hardware, and gripper were repurposed from older robotics parts, which kept the build cost down while making integration more constrained.

The competition framing emphasized payload delivery. The stated objective was to place bean bags and rings in different locations to gain as many points as possible and land the drone. The rules constrained the design toward short missions, one payload at a time, controlled flight, and safety-first operation.
