## Protecting the downstream rail

Power entry is where an otherwise correct system meets reversed connections, cable inductance, source variation, and transients. The goal of this circuit is to keep the protected rail inside a defined operating window without hiding the stress placed on the pass device.

The high-side NMOS is treated as an energy-handling component, not an ideal switch. Its gate trajectory, safe operating area, dissipation, and thermal path determine whether the protection concept survives the event it was designed for.

## Design reasoning

The useful analysis combines the transient waveform with the device limits. A design can clamp the output correctly and still fail if the NMOS crosses its safe operating area long enough to overheat.

> The output waveform is only half the result. The other half is the instantaneous voltage, current, and energy inside the transistor.

### Questions the calculations must answer

- What is the worst-case drain-to-source voltage?
- How much current flows during the limiting interval?
- Does the pulse remain inside the device SOA?
- How sensitive is the response to threshold variation?
- What is the steady-state power loss?
- What does the regulator see during startup and recovery?

## Simulation to bench

The simulation should produce expectations, not decorative plots. Bench validation then checks the gate, protected output, input surge, and load current on a shared timebase.

## Debugging story

The final case study should explicitly show where simulation and measurement disagree. Gate capacitance, source impedance, probe grounding, component tolerance, and thermal drift are all plausible sources—and each points toward a better model or a better circuit revision.
