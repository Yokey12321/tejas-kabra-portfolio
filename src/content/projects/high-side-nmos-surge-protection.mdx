## System requirements

This board protects a **12 V, 5 A load** when the input line jumps to **50 V for 50 ms**. The output cannot follow the input directly, and the pass devices, gate drive, and control electronics all have to survive the transient.

The circuit is a high-side linear protection stage. The main current path uses two back-to-back N-channel MOSFETs, while an op-amp feedback loop drives the MOSFET gates to regulate the protected output. In LTspice, when Vin rises to 50 V, Vout rises to about **13.8 V** before returning to 12 V after the pulse.

## Topology

The back-to-back MOSFET arrangement lets the board control the high-side current path while reducing conduction loss compared with a P-channel approach. The design uses **IPB120N10S403ATMA1** MOSFETs and treats the 50 V pulse as a short-duration linear-region stress case instead of only checking the steady-state current rating.

During the surge, the protected output sits near 14 V while the input is 50 V. That leaves about 36 V across the two series MOSFETs. Splitting that across the pair gives roughly **18 V per MOSFET**, and at 5 A that is approximately **90 W per MOSFET** for the 50 ms pulse. The design justification checked this against the MOSFET safe operating area and selected large devices with enough transient margin.

> The MOSFETs have to survive the pulse while the control loop maintains enough gate voltage to regulate.

## Gate drive and control rails

Because the MOSFETs sit on the high side, the source node rises near the protected output. That means the gate drive has to rise above the source instead of being driven from a normal 3.3 V or 5 V logic rail. A zener-limited 12 V control rail feeds an **LT1054 charge pump** so the op-amp can command a gate voltage in the roughly **18 V to 20 V** range under load.

The 12 V rail is generated from the input through a **680 ohm** resistor and a 12 V zener diode. During the 50 V surge, the resistor sees about 38 V. At roughly 55 mA, that puts resistor dissipation near **2.1 W**, so the design calls for a 2 W or higher part. The zener dissipation estimate lands around **0.24 W to 0.48 W**, so a 1 W to 2 W zener provides reasonable margin.

The low-voltage electronics use an **LM1117-3.3** regulator from the 12 V rail. At about 50 mA, the LDO dissipates roughly **0.435 W**, which is acceptable for the selected part and operating window.

## Feedback divider and clamp point

The op-amp compares the output sense voltage against the 3.3 V reference. A **32 k / 10 k** divider scales the output by:

<div className="data-grid">
  <div><small>Divider ratio</small><b>10 / (32 + 10) = 0.238</b></div>
  <div><small>Reference</small><b>3.3 V at the op-amp input</b></div>
  <div><small>Clamp target</small><b>3.3 / 0.238 = about 13.9 V</b></div>
</div>

That target explains the LTspice result. The output does not stay exactly at 12 V during the transient, but it clamps near the calculated regulation point, which keeps the load inside the intended operating range instead of exposing it to the full 50 V pulse.

## LTspice validation

The simulation model simplified some IC internals so the transient behavior could be tested quickly, but it preserved the important control relationship: Vin steps from 12 V to 50 V, the gate loop responds, and Vout remains near the clamp point.

## PCB implementation

The PCB layout keeps the high-current path visually obvious, with large copper regions for the MOSFET path and sense network, and smaller control circuitry grouped around the op-amp, charge pump, zener rail, LDO, MCU, oscillator, and CAN transceiver support blocks. Dense via stitching helps tie reference copper together and gives the power stage a more controlled return environment.

This page documents the main design steps: define the transient, choose the topology, estimate device stress, design the gate-drive supply, set the clamp point, simulate the pulse response, and route the board.
