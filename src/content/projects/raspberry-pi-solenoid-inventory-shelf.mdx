## System overview

Formula Electric electrical components were organized physically, but team members still had to search bins manually and track access separately. This project connects authenticated digital checkout to physical shelf access, so a user can identify themselves, select parts, and open the correct inventory location.

The system architecture combines a Raspberry Pi controller, touchscreen interface, card reader, 12 V solenoids, MOSFET driver boards, fused power distribution, and a CAD-packaged electronics enclosure. The reviewed electrical plan sized the actuator side around **15 pull-type solenoids** and a nominal **17.5 A at 12 V** system load.

## System architecture

The Raspberry Pi owns the user interface, authentication, checkout state, and hardware command logic. The software design review used an Electron front end with a Flask backend communicating to the hardware layer through PySerial or a similar serial interface. That keeps the UI and inventory logic on the high-level computer while the output hardware handles the solenoid switching.

The checkout flow was scoped around swipe or UIN authentication, scoped admin permissions, item and quantity selection, withdraw and deposit modes, and drawer-by-drawer access so only one location opens at a time. The software plan also included an endpoint exposing current item counts for live tracking through the operations system or app, plus low-stock notifications as a later feature.

## Power and actuator architecture

The power system is organized around a 12 V actuator rail and a lower-voltage logic/UI rail. The review schematic used an 18-channel 12 V PDU, individual 2 A fused branches for solenoids, a 5 A protected branch for the buck stage, and two 8-channel N-MOSFET boards to switch the solenoid outputs. The Pi, touchscreen, and card reader are powered from the stepped-down rail.

The current estimate treated the solenoids as the dominant load: **15 solenoids at about 1 A each**, plus about **2.3 A** for the buck converter feeding the Pi and screen, and about **0.2 A** for remaining logic and driver electronics. That puts the nominal system estimate near **17.5 A at 12 V**, or roughly **200 W**.

## Mechanical packaging

The CAD work focused on fitting the electrical hardware into the top of the shelf while keeping the drawer actuation hardware serviceable. The enclosure has to hold the controller, display, power distribution, driver boards, wire routing, and access hardware without interfering with the drawer rails.

The mechanical review identified several constraints that affected the design: the drawer rail prevented simple side protrusions, the shelf side walls were hollow and only about **1/16 in to 1/8 in** thick, and the top-side shelf structure was a better fastening location. Locking rods were planned as aluminum pieces connected to the solenoids, while the solenoid sheath was planned as a printed part with high infill. The electrical housing also needed a wire pass-through, with a neoprene grommet considered for routing roughly **30 wires** out of the enclosure.

## Integration and debugging

Integrated and debugged GPIO and I2C communication, solenoid actuation, power delivery, and driver-board behavior. Repeated mechanical operation exposed issues that do not appear in software-only testing, so reliability depended on validating the complete electrical and mechanical chain.

## Project outcome

The completed prototype allowed Formula Electric members to authenticate, select parts through the touchscreen, and access organized components without manually searching inventory bins. The strongest design work sits at the interface between software, power electronics, and mechanical packaging: the system had to know who was checking out parts, track quantities, and actuate physical drawers reliably.

The next additions should document the final installed hardware, touchscreen screenshots, measured solenoid current, driver-board behavior, checkout logs, and cycle-test results.

## Future improvements

A future revision would improve authentication by integrating NFC tags or a card-touch identification workflow. That would make team-member access faster and more repeatable than relying on a less integrated login path, while still keeping the checkout process tied to a specific user.

The mechanical enclosure also needs to be redesigned. The current printed housing is functional, but it is thin and flexible enough that it does not feel like a permanent shop installation. A stronger enclosure with better wall thickness, mounting features, cable strain relief, and protected electronics would make the system more secure and easier to maintain.

The largest electrical improvement would be replacing the purchased power-distribution board with a custom PDP designed around this exact shelf. The current system had to adapt around the bought board and use external buck converters to support the required rails. A custom board could combine distribution, regulation, connector placement, protection, and solenoid-driver support into a cleaner package. The goal is to compress the system, reduce wiring, remove unnecessary adapters, and make the electronics match the mechanical layout instead of forcing the layout around off-the-shelf power hardware.
