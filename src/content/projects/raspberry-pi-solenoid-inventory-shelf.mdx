## The problem

Formula Electric had physical storage for electrical components, but finding parts and tracking access were separate tasks. We wanted a member to identify themselves, select a component on a touchscreen, and open the right drawer.

I worked on the system design, software workflow, power integration, and mechanical packaging. The prototype combines a Raspberry Pi, touchscreen, card reader, 12 V solenoids, MOSFET drivers, and printed drawer-lock housings.

## From checkout to drawer access

The Raspberry Pi handles the interface, authentication, and checkout state. The software plan uses an Electron front end and Flask backend, with a serial connection to the hardware-control layer.

The workflow identifies a member by card or UIN, records the selected parts and quantities, and allows one drawer to open at a time. We also scoped administrator permissions, deposits and withdrawals, an inventory-count endpoint, and later low-stock notifications.

## Power and actuators

The design uses a 12 V actuator rail and a stepped-down rail for the Pi, display, and card reader. The electrical plan includes an 18-channel distribution board, **2 A fused solenoid branches**, a **5 A buck-converter branch**, and two Adafruit 8-channel N-MOSFET driver boards.

The full-load budget assumed **15 solenoids at roughly 1 A each**, approximately 2.3 A for the buck stage, and 0.2 A for the remaining electronics. That gives **17.5 A at 12 V**, or about **210 W**. This is a sizing estimate with all actuator loads counted, not a measurement of normal one-drawer-at-a-time operation.

## Packaging around the toolbox

The drawer rails and thin, hollow side walls limited where we could fasten the locking hardware. The top structure offered a better mounting location. I worked through the enclosure and bracket layout with those constraints in mind.

The enclosure had to fit the controller, touchscreen, distribution hardware, and drivers, while routing the actuator wiring clear of the drawers. The design considered a grommet for a pass-through carrying roughly 30 wires.

## Integration and the result

I worked on GPIO and I2C debugging, power delivery, solenoid switching, and driver-board behavior. Mechanical fit mattered as much as the software command: a successful electrical actuation still had to move the lock cleanly.

The completed prototype let members authenticate, select parts through the touchscreen, and access the corresponding storage. The installed hardware also made the next set of problems visible, especially the flexibility of the printed enclosure and the amount of wiring around the purchased distribution board.

## What I would change

I would strengthen the enclosure, improve its mounting points and strain relief, and make the electronics less accessible from outside. The existing housing works as a prototype, but it flexes more than I would want in a permanent shop installation.

The biggest electrical change would be a custom power-distribution board that combines regulation, protection, and connectors around this shelf's geometry. That would reduce the external buck converters, adapters, and wiring. NFC-based identification is another useful next step for the checkout workflow.

Before that revision, I would record actual solenoid current and repeatability over many drawer cycles. Those measurements would give the next design a firmer basis than the original power estimate.
